# Nighthawk Chain — Class slides

University of North Georgia · Supply chain / operations lab  
One class session. Copy each **Slide** heading into PowerPoint. Use the bullets on the slide. Keep *Speaker notes* in the notes pane.

Suggested deck: 8–10 slides, navy (#1F3D7C) background on title, cream (#F3EEE3) on content, gold (#FFC62F) for one accent rule. No UNG trademarks — this is a classroom lab, not an official mark.

This lab is the classic four-seat classroom supply-chain game (the same structure used in operations classes worldwide), restaged as cases of Nighthawk gear moving toward a UNG campus shop.

---

## Slide 1 — Title

**Nighthawk Chain**  
UNG Supply Chain Lab  
The bullwhip effect in one class session

- 15–20 minutes of play, plus a short brief and debrief
- Four seats: retailer · wholesaler · distributor · factory
- One decision each week: how many cases to order

*Speaker notes:* Open the lab on the projector. Create a room (one chain is enough to illustrate). Write the 4-letter code on the board. Students join on phones or laptops with a nickname. Extra people share a seat or watch.

---

## Slide 2 — Why we are playing

**Learning objectives**

- Feel lead time in a live chain, not a homework spreadsheet
- See how a small demand change grows as it moves upstream
- Connect a local inventory choice to the whole chain’s cost

*Speaker notes:* Do not lecture the bullwhip yet. Students should be slightly under-prepared. The debrief does the teaching. Keep this block under 8 minutes with seating.

---

## Slide 3 — The chain

**Shoppers → Retailer → Wholesaler → Distributor → Factory**

| Seat | Sells to | Orders from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retailer (campus store) | UNG campus shoppers | Wholesaler |
| Wholesaler | The retailer | Distributor |
| Distributor | The wholesaler | Factory |
| Factory | The distributor | Production line |

- Orders travel **upstream**
- Goods travel **downstream**
- You only see your own board. That is the design.

*Speaker notes:* Four students per chain is the classic setup. One chain on the projector is enough. Add more chains (Gainesville, Oconee, …) if the class is large.

---

## Slide 4 — A week of play

Each week, in this order:

1. **Receive** the shipment that left two weeks ago
2. **See demand** from your customer
3. **Ship** as much as you can from on-hand stock
4. **Backorder** anything you still owe
5. **Order** 0–30 cases (arrives in two weeks)

Starting board: 12 on hand, 4 arriving next week, recent demand of 4.

*Speaker notes:* Work one numeric example on the whiteboard. 12 + 4 arriving = 16 on hand. Customer wants 4. You ship 4. 12 left. Now order. Stress the delay: an order of 10 today is not sitting in your stock today.

---

## Slide 5 — Costs and the goal

- **$1** per case left on the shelf each week
- **$2** per case you still owe each week
- Lowest **combined** cost across the four seats wins

Do not hoard. Do not stock out. You will not do both perfectly.

*Speaker notes:* If they ask about demand: “It starts the way a quiet week on campus usually starts.” Do not mention Homecoming. Do not let seats talk about inventory.

---

## Slide 6 — How to log in

**Students:** open the site → Join a lab → 4-letter code + nickname → pick an open seat.

**You:** Instructor desk → create room → code on the board, PIN in your notes.

Empty seats become computer players when you press **Start week 1**. If a student freezes, order for them from the desk.

*Speaker notes:* Computers use a simple rule (order what just sold, plus a nudge toward 12 on hand). They are not optimal. They exist so a short chain can still run.

---

## Slide 7 — Play

**12 weeks. About a minute a week once they settle.**

Leave this slide up while they play. Watch the instructor desk:

- Who is waiting
- On-hand vs. backorders
- Orders that jump to 20+

*Speaker notes:* After week 4, stay quiet. Week 5 is Homecoming — shopper demand moves from 4 to 8 and stays there. Only the retailer sees it. Typical human reaction is to over-order. That is the lab.

---

## Slide 8 — Debrief the chart

Show the lab’s order chart (shoppers dashed, then retailer, wholesaler, distributor, factory).

Ask, in this order:

1. What did shoppers actually do?
2. Who felt the shortage first?
3. Who placed the largest order?
4. Did the factory’s orders look anything like shopper demand?

Name it: **the bullwhip effect** — order variance grows as you move upstream.

*Speaker notes:* Reveal demand after week 12. The only change was 4 → 8 in week 5. If factory orders hit 20–30, the whip is visible. Compare chains. Compare humans vs. a computer-only chain if you ran one.

---

## Slide 9 — Why it happens

- **Lead time** — you order for a future you cannot see
- **Hidden information** — only the retailer sees shoppers
- **Local targets** — covering *your* shelf over-corrects the chain
- **Panic** — a missed shipment becomes a bigger next order

*Speaker notes:* Optional real cases: toilet paper (2020), semiconductor allocations, promotional spikes in consumer goods. Ask: what would have dampened the whip? (share point-of-sale data, shorter lead time, a base-stock policy, not chasing last week’s stockout)

---

## Slide 10 — Takeaways

- A calm customer can still whip a factory
- More inventory is not automatically safer
- The cheap fix is visibility and a boring ordering rule
- Lowest team cost usually belongs to the chain that did *less*

## Rules of the game (leave in appendix)

- 12 weeks
- Roles: retailer, wholesaler, distributor, factory
- Lead time: 2 weeks
- Demand: 4 cases/week for weeks 1–4, then 8 cases/week (Homecoming) through week 12
- Start: 12 on hand, 4 in transit
- Costs: $1 holding, $2 backorder
- Orders: integers 0–30
- No sharing of inventory numbers during play
